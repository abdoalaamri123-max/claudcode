# Chapter 8: High-Throughput Count Data & Generalized Linear Models
*Source: Holmes & Huber, Modern Statistics for Modern Biology, https://www.huber.embl.de/msmb/08-chap.html*

## Core concepts

- **Counts are a statistical sample, not a census.** A sequencing/counting run measures a random subsample (`r` reads) of a much larger pool (`n` fragments); this sampling nature is the reason distributional models (not just descriptive stats) are needed.
- **Poisson is the naive per-library null.** If read `i` maps to gene `i` with probability `p_i = n_i/n` and reads are effectively independent (`n` in the billions/trillions ≫ `r` in the tens of millions, so sampling-without-replacement effects are negligible), the count for gene `i` in one library is `Poisson(λ_i)`, `λ_i = r·p_i`. This gives mean = variance = `λ_i` as the reference to check against (see Diagnostic checks).
- **Between biological replicates, variance exceeds Poisson — overdispersion is the norm, not the exception.** `p_i` itself fluctuates replicate-to-replicate (growth conditions, reagent amounts, timing), adding a second layer of variability on top of Poisson sampling noise. The standard fix is the **gamma-Poisson = negative binomial (NB)** distribution: two parameters per gene, mean `μ` and dispersion `α`, with `Var = μ + α·μ²` (Poisson is the special case `α→0`). Because raw counts span zero to millions with very different variance/shape at each scale (**heteroskedasticity**), a single global variance estimate is never appropriate — model mean and variance jointly, per feature.
- **"Normalization" = estimating and removing systematic sampling bias, not fitting a normal distribution.** The dominant bias is total library size / sequencing depth: two samples with identical true abundance for a gene will show different raw counts if their total read counts differ. Correct this via a per-sample **size factor** `s_j` before any cross-sample comparison.
  - Naive size factor = column sum of counts. Problem: dominated by whichever genes have the largest counts, so if even a few high-count genes are truly differentially expressed, the sum-based size factor is biased by them.
  - Better (median-of-ratios / robust-regression) estimator: for each gene, form the ratio of its count to a reference (e.g. geometric mean across samples); the size factor is a robust central tendency (median, or slope of a robust regression through the origin) of these per-gene ratios. This is far less sensitive to a handful of highly-expressed or DE genes. This is the DESeq2-style `estimateSizeFactorsForMatrix` approach; conceptually equivalent to edgeR's TMM. Python: `pydeseq2` implements median-of-ratios normalization; a crude proxy is CPM (`sc.pp.normalize_total`) but CPM is exactly the naive sum-based approach and inherits its bias.
  - Both normalization and dispersion estimation implicitly assume the **"few changes" assumption**: most genes are not differentially expressed (or changes are balanced up/down). If this is false (e.g., a global shift, or many genes moving in one direction — common in some perturbations, size-selected libraries, or heavily unbalanced cell-type compositions), estimate size factors/dispersion from a trusted negative-control/spike-in subset instead of all features.
- **GLM structure used for count-based differential testing** (conceptually — ignore R-specific syntax):
  `K_ij ~ GammaPoisson(μ_ij, α_i)`, `μ_ij = s_j · q_ij`, `log2(q_ij) = Σ_k x_jk β_ik`.
  I.e., raw counts `K` are modeled directly with a discrete, mean-variance-coupled distribution; the size factor `s_j` enters as a multiplicative offset (not a pre-division of the data); the systematic/biological effects enter through a **log link** acting on a linear combination of design-matrix columns, so fitted coefficients `β` are **log2 fold changes**. Python: `pydeseq2.DeseqDataSet` implements this whole pipeline; a from-scratch approach is `statsmodels.genmod.GLM` / `NegativeBinomial` with an explicit `offset=log(size_factor)` term (do NOT normalize the response and then feed it into a GLM expecting raw counts).
- **Design matrix encodes the science.** Binary/categorical/continuous covariates become columns; coefficients represent effects relative to a baseline (intercept). An **interaction term** (`x1·x2·β12`) captures non-additivity between two factors (log scale = multiplicative on the original scale) — only include it if you actually care whether combined treatment effects deviate from the sum of individual effects, and only if the design has samples in the combined condition.
- **Blocking/nuisance factors increase power when modeled, and can cause outright confounding when ignored.** Explicitly modeling a nuisance covariate (e.g., sequencing protocol, batch, cell line) lets its variability be absorbed into its own coefficient rather than into the residual/noise term — this can markedly increase detection power (in the book's worked example, 1325 vs 1061 genes significant at FDR 10% moving from 1-factor to 2-factor design, with only 25 genes lost and 289 gained). But: adding parameters also spends degrees of freedom, so the direction of the power change is data-dependent, not automatic.
- **Confounding**: if the factor of interest and a nuisance factor are not orthogonal/balanced (partially or fully confounded), no model can cleanly separate their effects — differences due to the nuisance factor get attributed to the factor of interest. This is a design problem statistics cannot fix after the fact; check balance (cross-tab factor levels) before trusting any result.
- **Shrinkage / empirical Bayes for dispersion and log fold changes.** With small replicate numbers (typical for designed experiments), per-gene maximum-likelihood dispersion and fold-change estimates are individually noisy. Fit a prior from the empirical distribution of per-gene MLEs across *all* genes (unimodal; peaked near the "typical" dispersion, and near 0 for fold changes), then shrink each gene's estimate toward that prior, weighted by how much information (precision) that gene's own data carries — noisy/low-count genes get pulled hard toward the prior, high-precision genes barely move. This is why low-count or low-replicate features should never be trusted at face-value fold change.
- **Point-null hypotheses become scientifically uninteresting at large sample size.** The null "effect is exactly zero" is almost never strictly true; with enough samples, trivially small, biologically meaningless effects become "significant." Prefer effect-size-aware tests: **banded/threshold (interval) null hypotheses** — test `|β| > θ` (greaterAbs), `|β| < θ` (lessAbs), `β > θ`, or `β < -θ` for a chosen minimum fold-change threshold `θ`, instead of only testing `β = 0`.
- **Robustness / breakdown point.** Least-squares fitting has breakdown point `1/n` (one bad point can arbitrarily distort all coefficients); the median has breakdown point `1/2`. Robust alternatives (least absolute deviations, M-estimation with a Huber-type loss, least trimmed/quantile squares, Cook's-distance-based downweighting) trade this fragility for reduced statistical efficiency when the model does hold — outlier handling is a bias/variance trade, not free power.
- **Transformation ≠ modeling.** GLMs test on raw counts using an appropriate discrete likelihood. Transformations (log, VST, rlog) are for *downstream* uses — visualization, clustering, PCA, general-purpose unsupervised methods — where a roughly homoskedastic, real-valued scale is convenient. Never substitute a transformed-data linear model/t-test for the count-based GLM as the primary differential test unless you have verified the transformation adequately stabilizes variance for your data.

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

- **Using Poisson (or any equal-mean-variance assumption) on data that show overdispersion.** Real biological replicates almost always have variance > mean beyond Poisson. Fitting a Poisson model (or a plain t-test on counts, or any method assuming constant/underestimated variance) understates the true noise, making p-values anti-conservative (false-positive-prone). Always check the mean-variance relationship empirically before trusting a Poisson or homoskedastic-Gaussian model.
- **Comparing raw counts across samples without normalizing for library size / total counts / sequencing depth.** Two samples with identical true biological abundance will yield different raw counts if their total depth differs — this alone can manufacture "differential expression." Always divide out (or offset by) a size factor before any cross-sample comparison; never treat raw counts as directly comparable across samples with different totals.
- **Using naive total-count (column-sum) normalization when a handful of high-count or strongly DE genes exist.** Sum-based size factors are skewed by those genes. Prefer a robust/median-of-ratios estimator; if in doubt, plot size-factor estimate vs. raw column sum — large disagreement is a warning sign, not just noise.
- **log(0) / naive pseudocount pitfalls.** `log2(n)` is undefined at `n=0`; the common fix `log2(n + 1)` (or `log2(n + n0)`) is an ad hoc choice with no principled way to pick the constant, and it inflates variance for weakly-detected (small but nonzero count) features — these show artificially high standard deviation compared to well-measured genes, which can distort clustering/PCA/heatmaps built on such data. Prefer a variance-stabilizing transformation (VST) or regularized-log (rlog)-style transform for downstream visualization/clustering; these have finite value and finite slope at count=0, and large counts converge to ordinary log2. In Python: no single canonical VST is universal — check `pydeseq2` for a DESeq2-equivalent VST, or apply `scanpy`'s default `sc.pp.normalize_total` + `sc.pp.log1p` (i.e., log1p of normalized counts) with explicit awareness that this is the naive-pseudocount case, not a stabilized one.
- **Ignoring a nuisance/blocking factor that has a real, detectable effect on the data.** If exploratory plots (heatmap, PCA) show samples clustering by a technical covariate (batch, protocol, processing day) rather than / in addition to the biological factor of interest, that covariate must be included in the model — omitting it either loses power (its variance leaks into the residual/noise term) or, if it is not balanced with the factor of interest, causes outright confounding (nuisance-factor effects misattributed to the biological effect — described in the source as "quite an embarrassment").
- **A covariate correlated with the outcome/test statistic acting as a hidden confounder — explicitly check for this.** An unmodeled but *orthogonal and balanced* nuisance variable still inflates the denominator of the test statistic and **depletes small p-values** (anti-conservative-in-reverse: too few discoveries, non-uniform p-value histogram) even though it isn't biasing the point estimate. Concretely worth checking on every DE/pseudobulk analysis: does **total counts / library size, cell number per (pseudobulk) sample, or detection rate** correlate with your outcome (mean expression, fold change) or with significance calls? If yes and it is not included as a covariate or corrected for, the result is confounded or underpowered — this is a known recurring failure mode in pseudobulk workflows (summed/mean expression per pseudobulk group tracking the number of cells contributing to that group), not a hypothetical concern.
- **Filtering low-count/low-information features using information derived from the same test being corrected for (e.g., filtering by p-value or observed fold change), then applying multiple-testing correction as if no filtering had occurred.** This breaks FDR control because the filter is not independent of the test statistic's null distribution — "double dipping." Correct ("independent filtering") procedure: choose a filter statistic that is independent of the test statistic *under the null* (typically, overall mean count/expression summed or averaged across all samples, ignoring group labels), apply it before or alongside testing but not derived from group-specific p-values/effect sizes, and only then run BH/FDR correction on the retained (tested) set. Filtering on total abundance is fine; filtering on "genes with p < 0.05" before FDR correction is not.
- **Treating a point-null significance call as biologically meaningful without checking effect size**, especially with many replicates — statistically significant but trivially small fold changes will accumulate. Report/threshold on effect size (log fold change) in addition to (or instead of) raw significance; consider a banded/threshold null hypothesis test if a minimum fold-change is scientifically required.
- **Letting a small number of outlier counts dominate a fitted model.** Least-squares-like fitting has a low breakdown point; a single extreme count (technical artifact, mapping error, rare real event) can distort a gene's fitted coefficients. Use a leverage/influence diagnostic (e.g., Cook's distance) to flag genes/samples where this is happening rather than accepting all fitted values uncritically.
- **Feeding already-normalized/derived values into a raw-count model, or raw counts into a model expecting normalized values.** The count-based GLM/NB likelihood is designed for actual raw integer read counts, with normalization handled as a multiplicative offset (`s_j`) inside the model — not by first dividing the data and then modeling the quotient as if it were still count-distributed. Conversely, don't skip normalization and directly compare/test raw counts across samples of different depth. Also applies to coverage/base-pair-derived or otherwise pre-processed quantities standing in for counts — these are not raw sampling counts and using a Poisson/NB model on them can be "nonsensical."
- **Assuming PCA/ordination axes automatically align with your covariate of interest.** The top principal components reflect whatever the dominant sources of variance are, technical or biological — always check whether PC1/PC2 track a *known* covariate (condition, batch, protocol) by coloring/annotating the plot, rather than assuming alignment.
- **Applying a Wald/t-type test without accounting for extra design-driven variance (Exercise 8.1 pattern).** An orthogonal, perfectly balanced batch variable that is NOT modeled still inflates the variance estimate used in the denominator of a per-feature test statistic, producing a p-value histogram *depleted* at small values (fewer discoveries than there should be) even though nothing is biased in expectation. Always include known orthogonal batch/blocking variables in the model even if "balanced," and diagnose via the p-value histogram shape.

## Assumptions to verify

- Is the sampling/independence approximation reasonable for this assay (large `n`, `r ≪ n`, many features so no single feature dominates)? — generally fine for bulk sequencing; verify for very low-input or highly skewed libraries.
- Does the **"few changes" assumption** (most features unchanged, or symmetric up/down) hold for this experiment? If a global shift or systematic imbalance is expected, don't use whole-dataset normalization/dispersion estimation — use a control/spike-in feature subset.
- Is a single per-gene dispersion (constant across samples/conditions) a reasonable simplification, or is dispersion expected to differ systematically between groups (e.g., very different sample sizes, different biological variability per condition)?
- Are the factor of interest and any nuisance/blocking factors (batch, protocol, processing day, cell line) **balanced/orthogonal** in the design? Cross-tabulate factor levels before analysis.
- Is the log link (multiplicative effects on the natural scale) the right functional form for this covariate's effect, or is an additive-on-raw-scale (identity link) or a different link more appropriate?
- Are replicates true biological replicates (independent noise sources) rather than pseudo-replicates (e.g., repeated measures on the same sample/library)?
- For any transformed-data downstream analysis (clustering, PCA, heatmap): does the transformation used (naive log-pseudocount vs. VST/rlog) actually flatten the mean-variance relationship for this dataset, or does it still show elevated variance at low counts?
- Is a covariate that's known/suspected to correlate with the outcome (total counts, cell number, detection rate, library size) included as a covariate/offset, or at minimum checked for correlation with the result?

## Diagnostic checks

- **Mean-variance plot.** Compute per-feature mean and variance across biological replicates (after size-factor adjustment), plot `log(variance)` vs `log(mean)`. Overlay reference lines of slope 1 (`v = m`, Poisson) and slope 2 (`v = c·m²`, quadratic/NB-dominated regime). Expect data to hug the slope-1 line at low means and bend toward slope-2 at high means; systematic deviation above even the slope-2 line signals additional, unmodeled overdispersion (extra covariate or QC issue).
- **Size factor vs. raw column sum scatter.** Compare a robust (median-of-ratios) size factor estimate to the naive column-sum estimate; near-proportional = fine; large systematic disagreement = a few high-count/DE genes are likely biasing the naive estimate.
- **p-value histogram.** Expect a uniform background across (0,1) plus a peak near 0 (true positives); rough FDR at a given bin ≈ (background level in that bin) / (peak height). A tilted/hump shape rising toward p=1, or depletion of small p-values, both indicate unmodeled batch/nuisance-driven extra variance — go back and add the suspected covariate to the model.
- **MA plot** (log fold change vs. mean normalized count). Check for asymmetry, banding of large fold-changes concentrated at low mean counts (a signature of noisy low-count estimates that shrinkage should be pulling in), and confirm points beyond an axis range are flagged rather than silently clipped.
- **PCA / ordination + heatmap on transformed (VST/rlog) data.** Check whether PC1/PC2 (or the dominant heatmap sample-clustering split) aligns with the biological covariate of interest, a suspected batch/nuisance factor, or something unexplained — any of the latter two is a signal to add that factor to the design.
- **Explicit confounder-correlation check** — correlate library size / total counts / cell number (for pseudobulk) / detection rate against both (a) the outcome/summary statistic being tested and (b) the resulting significance calls or fold changes. A strong correlation (rule-of-thumb concern territory: |ρ| ≳ 0.5, and especially the ~0.9 magnitude seen in some pseudobulk detection-mean setups) means the covariate should be added to the model or the summary statistic redefined, not ignored.
- **Cook's distance per gene/sample.** Default outlier cutoff: the 99th percentile of the `F(p, m−p)` distribution, where `p` = number of model parameters (incl. intercept) and `m` = number of samples. Genes above cutoff: either flag (set p-value/adjusted p-value to `NA`) if replicate number is small, or replace the outlier count with a size-factor-adjusted trimmed mean across samples if replicate number is large (conservative — cannot manufacture a false positive since it substitutes the null-hypothesis-predicted value).
- **Single-factor vs. multi-factor model comparison.** Fit both models (with and without a suspected nuisance covariate), compare p-values (scatter plot, e.g. on a compressed `(-log10 p)^(1/6)` scale) and the count of significant features at a fixed FDR threshold. A large shift is direct evidence the nuisance factor matters (for power, for confounding control, or both).
- **Balance table.** Cross-tabulate every pair of design factors (`condition × batch`, `condition × type`, etc.) — any non-uniform cell counts indicate a degree of confounding that no downstream modeling choice can fully undo.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Are raw (not pre-normalized, not derived) integer counts being fed into the count-based model, with normalization applied as a multiplicative size-factor/offset rather than pre-dividing the data?
- [ ] Has a size factor (library size / sequencing depth) been estimated and applied — ideally via a robust/median-of-ratios method rather than naive column sums?
- [ ] Has the mean-variance relationship been checked empirically (log-log plot vs. slope-1 and slope-2 references) rather than assumed to be Poisson?
- [ ] Is the dispersion parameter per-feature and, given likely small replicate numbers, shrunk toward a fitted trend/empirical-Bayes prior rather than used as raw per-gene MLE?
- [ ] Are all known nuisance/blocking factors (batch, protocol, processing day, cell line) included in the design, and is their balance with the factor of interest verified (not confounded)?
- [ ] Has a candidate confounding covariate — total counts, library size, cell number (pseudobulk), detection rate — been explicitly checked for correlation with the outcome and/or the significance calls?
- [ ] Is any low-count/low-expression filtering done using a criterion independent of the group-specific test statistic (e.g., overall mean), and applied consistently before FDR correction — not filtering on p-value/fold-change post hoc?
- [ ] Is FDR/multiple-testing correction applied only to the actually-tested (post-filter) feature set, using a proper procedure (e.g., BH), not a flat per-test threshold?
- [ ] Are log-transform pitfalls avoided — no naive `log2(n+1)` feeding into significance testing; VST/rlog-type transform used for visualization/clustering instead?
- [ ] Has the p-value histogram been inspected for the healthy shape (uniform + left peak), with tilting or depletion investigated as a batch-effect signal?
- [ ] Have outliers been assessed (e.g., Cook's distance) rather than trusting every fitted coefficient at face value?
- [ ] If sample size is large, has effect size (fold-change magnitude / banded null hypothesis) been considered alongside raw statistical significance?
- [ ] Do PCA/heatmap plots on transformed data confirm the top variance components align with expected covariates, not an unexplained or technical one?

## Key terms

- **Gamma-Poisson / negative binomial (NB)**: two-parameter (mean `μ`, dispersion `α`) discrete distribution used for overdispersed count data; `Var = μ + α·μ²`; Poisson is the `α→0` limit.
- **Overdispersion**: observed variance among replicates exceeding what a Poisson model predicts from the mean (`Var = mean`); the norm for biological replicate count data.
- **Size factor**: per-sample multiplicative scaling factor correcting for library size/sequencing depth and other systematic biases; applied as a GLM offset, not a pre-division.
- **Normalization** (in this context): estimating/removing systematic sampling biases (chiefly library size) — unrelated to the normal distribution.
- **Median-of-ratios normalization**: robust size-factor estimator using a per-gene ratio to a reference (e.g. geometric mean), aggregated via median/robust regression; resistant to a few highly-expressed or DE genes, unlike a naive column-sum estimator.
- **Heteroskedasticity**: variance that changes systematically with the mean/magnitude of the data — the norm for raw counts across their dynamic range.
- **Generalized linear model (GLM)**: linear model generalized via (a) a link function mapping the linear predictor to the outcome's natural range, and (b) an arbitrary error/sampling distribution (not necessarily normal), fit by maximum likelihood.
- **Link function**: transformation connecting the linear predictor to the mean of the outcome distribution; log2 link used for count GLMs (coefficients = log2 fold changes).
- **Design matrix**: matrix encoding experimental factors/covariates as columns and samples as rows; the "scientific content" of a linear/generalized linear model.
- **Interaction term**: coefficient capturing non-additive combined effects of two or more factors (multiplicative deviation on the natural scale).
- **Blocking factor / nuisance covariate**: a variable not of primary interest but modeled to absorb its variance and avoid confounding (e.g., batch, protocol).
- **Confounding**: when the factor of interest and a nuisance factor are not balanced/orthogonal, so their effects cannot be statistically separated.
- **Shrinkage / empirical Bayes**: estimating a prior from the ensemble of per-feature MLEs (dispersions, fold changes) and pulling each feature's individual estimate toward it, weighted by that feature's own precision.
- **Breakdown point**: fraction of data points that must be corrupted to make an estimator arbitrarily wrong; least-squares mean ≈ `1/n`, median ≈ `1/2`.
- **Cook's distance**: influence/leverage diagnostic per data point on fitted regression coefficients; used to flag/handle count outliers.
- **Variance-stabilizing transformation (VST)**: data transform designed to make variance roughly constant across the dynamic range (finite value/slope at count=0, ≈log2 at high counts); for visualization/clustering, not for the primary count-based test.
- **Regularized logarithm (rlog)**: shrinkage-based alternative to VST, better behaved when samples have very different size factors.
- **Banded / interval null hypothesis**: testing `|β|` or `β` against a nonzero fold-change threshold `θ` rather than only against exactly 0 — addresses the "point null is almost never exactly true" critique.
- **Independent filtering**: removing low-information features via a criterion independent of the test statistic's null distribution (e.g., overall mean) prior to/alongside testing, so that multiple-testing correction on the retained set remains valid.
- **MA plot**: mean (normalized count) vs. log2 fold-change scatter plot; standard first-look differential-expression diagnostic.
