# Chapter 13: Design of High-Throughput Experiments
*Source: Holmes & Huber, Modern Statistics for Modern Biology, https://www.huber.embl.de/msmb/13-chap.html*

## Core concepts

- **Types of experiments, in decreasing order of control:**
  - **Controlled experiment**: researcher controls system, environment, and treatment assignment (e.g., cell line, defined media, precise drug dose).
  - **Study**: some relevant conditions (weather, patient background) are not under the researcher's control.
  - **Observational study**: even the variable of interest (e.g., treatment/exposure group) is not assigned by the researcher. Prone to confounding; "correlation is not causation" applies directly. Can be prospective or retrospective.
  - **Randomized controlled trial (RCT)**: researcher does not control all nuisance factors but *does* control assignment of the variable of interest; with enough sample size nuisance effects average out and effects can be causally attributed. Normally **prospective** (outcome unknown at assignment time).
  - **Meta-analysis**: observational study of previous studies; increases effective sample size and can average out study-level biases (but inherits each study's confounds if systematic).
  - When reviewing an analysis, first identify which of these it is — an observational-study result is being asked to support a causal claim more often than it should.

- **Error decomposes into noise and bias.** **Noise** averages out with more replicates. **Bias** does not — it can even become *more apparent* with more replication (tight cloud centered on the wrong value). Bias is harder to catch than noise: you cannot see it just by looking at within-replicate spread; you need an explicit model or external check to detect and correct it.
- **ANOVA-style decomposition**: total sum of squares `C_total = C_within_groups + C_between_groups`. This split is not unique — what counts as "within-group noise" at one level of grouping can become "between-group signal" once the right subgroups (e.g., batch, sex, cell type) are identified. If an analysis lumps a real subgroup structure into "noise," it is throwing away identifiable signal (and inflating its noise estimate).
- **Batch effects** = *known/recorded* systematic factors (reagent lot, day, operator, machine) that bias measurements. **Latent factors** = *suspected but unrecorded* systematic factors; in high-dimensional data, noise from latent factors tends to be *correlated across features*, which can cause faulty inference if ignored — but the same correlation structure can be exploited to estimate and remove it (surrogate variable analysis, mixed/latent-factor models).
- **Effect size** = difference between group centers (mean or median). Increasing sample size increases the *precision* with which the effect size is estimated (statistical power to detect a group difference), but does **not** improve the performance of the same measurement as a per-sample diagnostic/classifier — that depends on the within-group (spread) distributions, which more replicates do not shrink.
- **Effective sample size**: a sample of `n` independent observations is more informative than `n` correlated ones. If measurements come in correlated clusters (e.g., neighborhood, litter, patient, tissue block), the *effective* n is smaller than the raw count of measurements, and naive formulas that assume independence understate the true variance of the estimate.
- **Blocking**: hold a nuisance factor constant *within* each block and only vary the treatment of interest within it (Darwin's paired cross-/self-pollinated maize in the same pot = block). Paired designs are the simplest case of blocking. **"Block what you can, randomize what you cannot."** (George Box, 1978)
- **Randomization**: for nuisance factors you cannot block, randomize their assignment relative to the variable of interest so they average out over enough replicates. Randomization also protects against *unconscious handling bias* — e.g., being more careful with rare/precious samples from one group than the other, which can itself bias the measured outcome.
- **Balanced design**: every combination of factor levels has the same number of replicates; this keeps each factor's effect identifiable. Deviating from balance (common for logistical/economic reasons) puts inference "on thin ice" — proceed with caution and check confounding explicitly.
- **Power calculation** always involves four linked quantities: sample size, effect size, significance level (α, false-positive rate), and power (true-positive rate = P(reject H0 | HA true)). Fixing any three determines the fourth. Conventional target: **power ≥ 0.80**. Python: `statsmodels.stats.power` (`TTestIndPower`, `TTestPower` for paired, `FTestAnovaPower`, `NormalIndPower`, `GofChisquarePower`) mirror the R `pwr` package's `pwr.t.test`, `pwr.anova.test`, `pwr.chisq.test`.
- **Paired/blocked designs are dramatically more powerful when the blocking factor's variance is large.** Book's own simulation (n=15/group, effect=0.2, noise sd=0.25): paired t-test power ≈ 0.56 vs unpaired ≈ 0.001 when a shared "pot" effect (sd=0.5) dominates the noise. Worked `pwr.t.test` example: to reach 80% power at d=0.4, unpaired needs **n≈99/group**, paired needs **n≈51 pairs** — roughly half the sample size for the same power, because pairing removes the shared nuisance variance from the comparison. When pot/block effects are small relative to measurement noise, pairing barely helps — the benefit of blocking scales with how much of the total variance the blocking factor explains, not with sample size alone.
- **Effect size in 'omics screens is usually a guess.** With thousands of features tested at once, you rarely know the true effect size in advance ("the elephant in the room"). Power calculations are still useful for order-of-magnitude planning and for *relative* comparisons (e.g., paired vs. unpaired, design A vs. design B) even when the absolute effect size is uncertain.
- **Mean-variance relationships** (before any transform), three prototypes: (1) constant, `v(m) = c` (variance independent of mean); (2) Poisson-like, `v(m) = a·m` (variance ∝ mean); (3) quadratic, `v(m) = b·m²` (sd ∝ mean). Real assays often mix these, e.g. microarray intensities: `v(m) = b·m² + c` (additive background noise + multiplicative signal-proportional noise) — multiplicative term dominates at high signal, background dominates at low signal.
- **Variance-stabilizing transformations** (log, `asinh`, DESeq2/edgeR-style) matter because they: improve visualization (a plot/heatmap isn't dominated visually by a few huge values); improve PCA/clustering (which would otherwise be dominated by a handful of highly-expressed/high-intensity features); improve validity of models/tests that assume homoskedastic (equal-variance) noise.
- **QA vs QC**: quality **assessment** = measuring/monitoring data quality (marginal/joint distributions, replicate agreement, evidence of batch effects); quality **control** = actually removing bad data. Quality itself is best defined as **"fitness for purpose,"** not adherence to a generic spec sheet (e.g., read-length/Q-score metrics matter only insofar as they predict your ability to detect the biological effect you actually care about).
- **Sufficiency / "leaky pipelines."** Multi-stage pipelines (raw signal → calls → alignment → counts → normalization → summarization) necessarily compress information at each step. Check that each summarization step retains what the *next* analysis step needs (e.g., differential-expression testing needs raw counts, not pre-normalized/rounded values) — a pipeline assembled from independently-built components is especially prone to silently losing required information partway through.
- **"Analyze as you go" (dailies).** Don't wait until all data are collected to start looking at it — intermediate QC/visualization catches protocol drift, mislabeling, and batch problems while they can still be fixed, rather than post-mortemed. Fisher: *"To consult the statistician after an experiment is finished is often merely to ask him to conduct a post mortem examination."*
- **Reproducibility infrastructure** (language-agnostic principles): literate programming/notebooks over disconnected scripts + README files; version control (git); centralize and read-lock raw data, keep derived data in a separate, regenerable location; wrap recurring logic in functions/modules, not copy-paste; automate the raw→derived data pipeline so it can be re-run end to end.

## Common mistakes / pitfalls

- **Confounding biological signal with batch/technical variables.** Canonical failure mode (book's own figure): a biomarker looks like it cleanly separates disease vs. healthy — until you learn the data were acquired in two batches (different labs/machines/timepoints) and the batches align with the disease/healthy split. Conclusion: **"It is impossible to know from these data: the two variables are confounded."** No statistical fix recovers this after the fact — it requires a design that decouples the two variables. Explicitly check: **is there a nuisance variable (batch, processing day, operator, reagent lot, section/core, extraction date) that is correlated with both (a) group assignment and (b) the outcome being measured?** If yes, the biological effect and the nuisance effect are not separable from these data alone.
- **Confounding can be subtle, not just "batch 1 = cases, batch 2 = controls."** A biomarker might correlate with disease status only because it's a marker of a lifestyle factor that also causes the disease, or of inflammation caused by the disease *and* many other things. Don't assume confounding is only about obvious technical variables — ask what else could co-vary with the grouping variable.
- **Historical example of confounding by design flaw**: Darwin's maize cross-/self-pollination experiment systematically placed cross-pollinated plants on the same side of every pot — Fisher pointed out this confounds a "side of pot" effect (e.g., differential sunlight) with the pollination-type effect being studied. Fix would have been to randomize which side got which treatment.
- **Pseudoreplication: mistaking technical replicates for biological replicates.** Canonical worked example: (a) one person weighed 20 times on a milligram-precision scale vs. (b) ten different people weighed once each on ordinary bathroom scales — despite "20 replicates" beating "10 replicates" on paper, design (a) tells you almost nothing about whether the drug generalizes to *people*, because all 20 measurements share the same biological unit; design (b), even though noisier per-measurement, is the one that can support a population-level claim. **The number of replicates matters far less than what source of variation each replicate actually captures.** Re-measuring the same tissue section, the same culture well, the same animal, or the same library prep multiple times is *technical* replication — it quantifies instrument/assay noise, not biological variability, and must not be plugged into a test's `n` as if it were independent biological evidence. Generalization to a wider population is only licensed by replication *at the level the claim is being made about* (patient, animal, litter, independent culture).
- **Treating correlated measurements as independent inflates apparent precision.** Effective-sample-size simulation: polling `n` people at `n` independent locations vs. polling `n` people clustered in groups of 3 neighbors — same raw `n`, but the clustered-sampling estimate has visibly higher variance/spread because neighbors' opinions are correlated. Any analysis that computes SE/df from the raw count of measurements without accounting for such clustering (patients × sections, wells × plates, cells × individual) will report artificially small p-values / narrow CIs.
- **Underpowered "me-too" studies.** Running a study without an a priori power calculation (or with an implausibly optimistic assumed effect size) risks a study that is essentially incapable of detecting the effect of interest even if it is real — check this before, not after, running the experiment.
- **Not blocking/pairing when a natural pairing exists.** If samples have an obvious shared confound (same patient before/after, same litter, same batch/pot), and the analysis uses an unpaired/unblocked test, statistical power is left on the table (in the book's simulation, roughly half the effective sample size is wasted) and residual nuisance variance dilutes the comparison.
- **Failure to randomize processing order.** If sample handling order is not randomized with respect to group assignment (e.g., all controls processed first, all cases processed last), any time-dependent drift (reagent degradation, instrument calibration drift, operator fatigue/learning) becomes confounded with the group comparison.
- **Lack of blinding / unconscious differential handling.** Treating rare or hard-to-obtain samples "extra carefully" relative to other samples introduces a systematic, unconscious bias into the measurement itself, independent of any biological effect.
- **Ignoring correlated latent noise in high-dimensional data.** In high-dimensional (many-feature) data, unmodeled latent factors tend to induce *correlated* noise across features, which is a more dangerous form of confounding than independent per-feature noise — it can create or mask apparent structure that has nothing to do with the biology of interest (this is exactly the mechanism behind spurious PC axes and gene-set "signals" driven by a technical covariate).
- **Misplaced "objectivity"**: treating all hypotheses/features as exchangeable/equally likely a priori (uniform multiple-testing correction, unweighted feature standardization, uninformed clustering) when real prior information about power or prior probability of a true effect exists and should be used to sharpen inference — pretending not to know things you actually know is not rigor, it's waste.

## Assumptions to verify

- Is the design actually a controlled experiment / RCT, or an observational study being interpreted as if causal claims were licensed?
- Is any nuisance/technical variable (batch, day, operator, reagent lot, plate, section, extraction date, scanner) correlated with the group variable of interest? (Cross-tabulate group × suspected nuisance factor — an unbalanced or perfectly aligned table is the smoking gun.)
- Are the "replicates" in the analysis truly independent biological units, or are some of them repeated/technical measurements on the same biological unit (same patient, same tumor, same well, same library)? What is the actual unit of biological replication for the claim being made?
- Was sample-processing order randomized with respect to group assignment?
- Was handling blinded to group assignment, or could differential care/attention have been applied to one group?
- Is the design balanced across nuisance factors, or does an imbalance exist that needs explicit adjustment/reporting?
- Was a power/sample-size calculation done before data collection, using a defensible (pilot-data- or literature-based) effect size — or was the sample size just "whatever was convenient"?
- Does a natural pairing/blocking structure exist (same subject at two time points, matched controls, same pot/litter) that the analysis is failing to exploit?
- Does the mean-variance relationship of the raw data match the assumptions of the test/model being used (constant, Poisson-like, quadratic)? Is a variance-stabilizing transform needed?
- At each pipeline stage, is the information the next stage needs (e.g., raw counts for count-based DE testing) still present, or has an earlier "convenience" summarization already discarded it?

## Diagnostic checks

- **Confounding check (do this first, always):** for any covariate available (batch, day, operator, cell count, detection rate/efficiency, RNA/protein quality, section, core, plate position, library size), compute its correlation/association with (a) the group/treatment variable and (b) the outcome variable. A covariate correlated with *both* is a candidate confounder — flag it explicitly and either stratify, adjust, or refuse to draw a causal conclusion. This generalizes the book's disease-vs-batch figure to any dataset.
- **Stratified re-plot**: re-draw the key comparison (boxplot/scatter) colored/faceted by the suspected nuisance factor (batch, processing day, block). If the apparent group effect disappears or reverses within strata, the original effect was confounded, not real (mirrors the book's paired disease/batch and blocking figures).
- **Cross-tabulate design factors**: build the group × batch (× operator × day) contingency table before analysis. Any row/column that is entirely one group is a fatal confound with no statistical remedy; a merely unbalanced table needs explicit adjustment (mixed model with batch as covariate/random effect) or should be flagged as a caveat.
- **Effective-n / clustering check**: identify the true independent sampling unit (patient, animal, culture) and confirm that degrees of freedom / n in the test match the count of independent units, not the count of technical replicates or repeated measurements. If measurements are clustered (multiple sections/cores per patient, multiple cells per sample), use a model that accounts for the clustering (mixed-effects model, cluster-robust SE, or aggregate to one value per biological unit before testing) rather than a plain t-test/ANOVA on all raw measurements.
- **Power via simulation**: simulate data under a plausible effect size and noise model (drawn from pilot data or the literature) and compute the empirical rejection rate across many simulated replicates; compare to the 0.80 convention. Useful even in 'omics settings where the exact effect size is unknown, for order-of-magnitude and relative (design A vs. B) comparisons.
- **Paired vs. unpaired power comparison**: if a natural blocking/pairing variable exists, simulate both a paired and unpaired test on the same data-generating process; if pairing measurably increases power, the analysis should use it.
- **QA plots for batch/technical structure**: PCA, ordination, or heatmap of samples colored by every known technical covariate (batch, day, operator, plate, lane) as well as by the biological variable of interest. A technical covariate that aligns with (or dominates) the leading PCs is evidence of a batch effect that could be masking or mimicking biology.
- **Mean-variance plot**: plot per-feature (or per-condition) variance against mean; check which of the constant/Poisson/quadratic regimes (or a mixture) the data follow, and whether the planned transform/test's variance assumption matches.
- **Replicate agreement check**: compare technical replicates to each other and to biological replicates/different conditions — replicate agreement should be visibly tighter than between-condition differences; if not, technical noise may be swamping the biological signal you're trying to detect.

## Quick checklist

- [ ] Classify the design: controlled experiment / study / observational study / RCT / meta-analysis — and don't let causal language exceed what the design licenses.
- [ ] Cross-tabulate group assignment against every known technical covariate (batch, day, operator, plate, reagent lot, extraction date). Flag any confounding (partial or total).
- [ ] Check any candidate covariate for correlation with **both** group assignment and outcome — that combination is the signature of a confounder, not a legitimate finding.
- [ ] Identify the true unit of biological replication (patient/animal/independent culture) and confirm the test's `n`/df counts independent units, not technical replicates (sections, cores, wells, re-scans, repeated library preps from the same sample).
- [ ] If data are clustered (multiple measurements per biological unit), use a method that accounts for it (mixed model, per-unit aggregation, cluster-robust SE) — not a naive test that treats all measurements as independent.
- [ ] Confirm sample-processing order was randomized with respect to group.
- [ ] Confirm handling/scoring was blinded, or assess whether differential handling could have occurred.
- [ ] Confirm a power/sample-size justification exists, with a stated effect size and its source (pilot data, literature, or explicit assumption) — target power ≥ 0.80.
- [ ] If natural pairing/blocking exists (same subject, matched samples, same batch/litter), confirm the analysis exploits it (paired test / mixed model with block term) rather than discarding it.
- [ ] Check whether the analyzed quantity's mean-variance relationship matches the test's assumptions; apply a variance-stabilizing transform if not.
- [ ] Verify no pipeline stage has silently discarded information (e.g., raw counts) that a downstream step requires.
- [ ] Prefer "analyze as you go" — confirm QC/visualization happened during data collection, not only after all data were in.

## Key terms

- **Controlled experiment / study / observational study / RCT**: decreasing levels of researcher control over conditions and/or treatment assignment; only RCTs (and true controlled experiments) license straightforward causal interpretation.
- **Noise vs. bias**: noise averages out with replication; bias persists (and can appear more precisely wrong with more replicates).
- **Batch effect**: a known/recorded systematic technical factor that biases measurements (reagent lot, day, machine, operator).
- **Latent factor**: an unrecorded/unknown systematic factor suspected to bias measurements; often correlated across features in high-dimensional data.
- **Confounding**: two variables (typically one biological, one technical/nuisance) co-vary across samples such that their individual effects on the outcome cannot be statistically separated.
- **Technical replicate**: repeated measurement of the *same* biological unit/material (re-run, re-section, re-scan); quantifies assay/instrument noise only.
- **Biological replicate**: independent biological unit (different patient, animal, litter, independent culture); required to support any claim about generalization beyond the specific unit measured.
- **Pseudoreplication**: analyzing technical replicates (or any non-independent repeated measures) as if they were independent biological replicates, inflating the effective n and understating variance/p-values.
- **Effect size**: difference between group centers (mean/median); what power calculations are calibrated against.
- **Effective sample size**: the number of *independent* pieces of information in a dataset, which can be less than the raw count of measurements when observations are correlated/clustered.
- **Power**: P(reject H0 | HA true); conventional design target ≥ 0.80; a function of sample size, effect size, and significance level.
- **Blocking**: restricting comparisons to within groups (blocks) that share a nuisance factor, so that factor cancels out of the treatment comparison.
- **Pairing / matched design**: the simplest form of blocking — same subject (or a closely matched subject) measured under both conditions.
- **Balanced design**: equal replicate counts across all factor-level combinations.
- **Randomization**: random assignment of treatment/order/handling with respect to nuisance factors, so their effects average out and unconscious bias is prevented.
- **Mean-variance relationship**: how measurement variance scales with the mean signal (constant / Poisson-like / quadratic, or a mixture); motivates variance-stabilizing transforms.
- **Quality assessment (QA) vs. quality control (QC)**: measuring/monitoring data quality vs. actively removing bad data; quality itself = fitness for purpose.
- **Sufficiency / leaky pipeline**: whether a data-summarization step retains all information a downstream analysis step needs; multi-stage pipelines risk silently losing required information at some stage.
