# Rebuttal Strategy and Phrase Bank

Read this alongside `references/difficult-cases.md` when drafting Step 4 (the point-by-point) and Step 6 (hard comments) of the Draft workflow. `difficult-cases.md` covers how to *handle* specific hard-comment categories; this file covers the persuasion mindset behind every response, a playbook keyed by comment type, and a bank of ready-to-use phrasing. It applies across all comments, not only the hard ones.

## Core mindset: persuasion, not self-defense

A reviewer's written comment is only the conclusion; the reasoning behind it is often left implicit. Before drafting each response, work out which of these is actually driving the comment, since the right response differs for each:
- The reviewer is genuinely unconvinced by the evidence.
- Unclear writing caused a misreading of what was actually done.
- The reviewer is unfamiliar with this specific subfield's norms.

## Know your true audience

The response is read by all reviewers and the editor, not only the reviewer who wrote the comment. Every response should do three things at once: address the specific reviewer's point, signal scientific rigor and good faith to the editor, and, where a comment is weak or unfair, quietly reduce its credibility in the editor's eyes without attacking the reviewer.

## Response playbook by comment type

- **Novelty attack.** Restate the core contribution, clarify it is not a trivial combination of existing pieces, and re-express the key insight from a different angle. If one component is called underwhelming, shift emphasis to a stronger component or the overall paradigm, or argue that a simple-but-effective, clearly distinct approach is itself the contribution.
- **Factual error, and the reviewer is actually right.** Concede cleanly, thank the reviewer, and commit to the fix.
- **Factual error caused by a misreading of unclear writing.** Concede that the description was unclear, state plainly what was actually done, and point to the revised text.
- **Insufficient improvement/gain, no comparison cited.** Benchmark the reported gain against comparable published state-of-the-art results to show it sits in a normal range for the field.
- **Insufficient gain, with a specific comparison cited.** Identify the differences in experimental conditions (platform, input scale, settings) that make the cited comparison unfair.
- **Insufficient experiments.** If feasible, run them this round; journals generally expect major-revision requests to be met in the current round, and promising results only for the final version reads as a weak response and should be avoided where avoidable. For genuinely unreasonable requests, explain plainly why the experiment is unnecessary or out of scope.

## Cross-cutting rules

- **Answer every point.** Never silently drop a comment. If space is tight, merge similar comments and answer them together, but do not omit one.
- **Concede vs. push back is a cost-benefit call.** Accept small asks (a wording change, one added figure, a narrowed claim) to build goodwill; reserve pushback for high-cost asks that are genuinely out of scope, and justify the pushback with reasons.
- **Use conflicting reviews to your advantage.** If one reviewer's criticism is contradicted by another reviewer's praise on the same point, quote the positive review to offset the negative one, for the editor's benefit.
- **Concentrate effort on the negative reviewer(s).** Secure the positive reviewer's points without over-investing time there.
- **Correct a reviewer's factual error early and politely**, to establish the initiative for the rest of the response (for example, a request for a non-standard benchmark).
- **Abbreviate established terms** to save space where a response is tight for room.

## Make the response self-contained

Reviewers often do not reread the full manuscript in later rounds; they read the response. For each point, paste the revised text directly into the response rather than citing only a line number, so the reviewer can verify the change without opening the manuscript. This is the same principle behind this skill's quoted-text style (Step 4 of the Draft workflow): quoting beats pointing wherever the target journal allows it.

## Deliverables and structure

A rebuttal package is typically thought of as three parts: the point-by-point response letter, the cover letter to the editor summarizing how the major concerns were handled, and the tracked-changes manuscript. (This skill's own Output package already produces all of these, plus a summary of revisions and an evaluation checklist as extra rigor beyond the typical minimum — this section reinforces, not replaces, that package.) Open the point-by-point with a short summary of the major changes, and visually separate each reviewer's comment from the response (this skill's default is the blue-comment/black-response convention already described in Step 4).

## Editor confidential comments

When a reviewer is clearly unqualified for the subject matter, unfamiliar with the field's norms, biased, or self-contradictory, note this in the confidential comments to the editor (the channel invisible to reviewers) so the editor can weigh that review accordingly. Keep the note polite and about the argument, not the person. This extends the same principle already covered in `references/difficult-cases.md`'s section on a reviewer conflict of interest or inappropriate feedback: raise the concern to the editor privately, never inside the public point-by-point.

## Handling conflicts and editor priority

For directly conflicting reviewer requests, do not silently pick a side: state the disagreement explicitly in both responses, give the reasoning, and defer the final call to the editor (see `references/difficult-cases.md`'s conflicting-reviewers section for the specific mechanics). The editor's own comments carry the highest priority of anything in the letter and should get a dedicated, thorough response, not folded into a reviewer's section.

## Tone

Reserve sincere thanks for comments that were genuinely valuable; a blanket thank-you to every comment reads as hollow. Keep disagreement about the work, not the person: "the reviewer may have overlooked Table X" rather than "the reviewer is wrong."

## Phrase bank

Ready-to-use phrasing, organized by the move each sentence makes. Adapt to the specific comment; do not paste unmodified filler where a specific answer is needed.

### Opening

- Thank you for your suggestion.
- Thank you for the positive/detailed/constructive comments.
- We sincerely thank all reviewers and the editor for their time and effort. Below we respond to each specific comment.
- We thank the reviewers for their useful comments. We first address the common questions, then respond to each individual review.
- We appreciate the opportunity to revise our manuscript and have addressed all points raised. A summary of the major changes precedes the point-by-point responses below.

### Agreeing / conceding

- We thank the reviewer for pointing out this issue.
- We agree and have incorporated this suggestion throughout the manuscript.
- We have addressed this comment by [action].
- We have added/compared/revised/corrected [X] in the revised manuscript (see [location]).
- This is a valid point. We have [action] accordingly, and the revised text now reads: "[pasted revised text]".

### Disagreeing / pushing back

- We respectfully disagree with the reviewer that [X], for the following reason.
- The reviewer may have overlooked [Table/Figure/Section X], which shows that [Y].
- We could compare with [X]; however, it is not directly related to our work because [Y].
- We would like to emphasize that [X].
- The reviewer raises an interesting concern. However, our work [X], and therefore [Y].
- Thank you for the comment, but we cannot fully agree. As stated in [location], [X].
- This is an important point; however, we believe [X] would be outside the scope of this study because [Y].
- This is a fair assessment of [X]; however, we believe [Y] would be more appropriate because [Z].

### Clarifying

- We had indeed stated/included/discussed/reported [X] in the original manuscript (see [location]).
- As stated in [location], [X].
- We have rewritten [section] to better align with the reviewer's comment. We hope the edited text clarifies [X]. It now reads: "[pasted revised text]".
- We apologize that our original description was unclear. What we actually did is [X]; we have revised the text to make this explicit.

### Adding information / deferring

- We have added a new figure/table (see [location]) to further illustrate [X].
- We have supplemented the [section] with an explanation of [X].
- Thank you for the comment. We agree this is an interesting direction and will explore it in future work.
