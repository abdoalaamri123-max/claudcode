# Appeal Mode

Read this when the paper was rejected and the user wants to contest the decision. The job is to draft a formal appeal (rebuttal) letter to the handling editor, not a response-to-reviewers letter. Appeal mode is the one context in this skill where a reasoned rebuttal of an editorial decision is appropriate, but it must stay formal, respectful, and non-combative throughout, exactly as the revision philosophy of "engage, do not rebut" requires everywhere else.

## Mindset before appealing

Rejection is routine, not a signal of low-quality work — it happens to everyone, including established researchers. Don't resent the referees' comments, and remember there are other journals; a rejection at one venue is rarely the end of the road for the science itself. Sleep on a rejection before deciding how to respond: assess the case realistically once the initial reaction has passed, rather than drafting while still stung by it.

## When to appeal (grounds)

An appeal is justified only on real, checkable grounds:

- A clear factual error or misunderstanding in the decision letter or in a review.
- A review that missed or misread key content already present in the manuscript.
- Procedural unfairness in how the manuscript was handled.
- A demonstrable reviewer error (a wrong calculation, a misattributed claim, a citation that says the opposite of what the reviewer states).
- New data or additional experiments performed since the original submission that materially change the picture — genuinely new work, not a re-argument of the same evidence already considered.

An appeal is not justified by mere disagreement with the outcome, a wish for another chance, or re-arguing subjective editorial judgments such as novelty, scope, priority, or fit with the journal. Those calls belong to the editor and are rarely overturned on appeal.

Set realistic expectations before drafting: appeal success rates are low at most journals, and the process costs time the author could spend resubmitting elsewhere. Whether to appeal is the author's decision, not the drafter's. Lay out the case for and against clearly (grounds strength, time cost, alternative of resubmitting elsewhere) rather than steering the author toward appealing.

Appeals following a rejection without external review (a desk rejection) are less likely to succeed than appeals of a post-review rejection: the same editor who made the original call will typically see the appeal too, and appeals generally do not get high priority in an editor's queue. Weigh this into the realistic assessment above, especially for a desk rejection.

If appealing is not the right call, or an appeal is filed and denied, the next step is choosing a new target journal (`journal-selector`) and drafting a fresh first-submission cover letter for it (`cover-letter-writer`) — not reusing this appeal letter, and not repurposing the original response-to-reviewers cover letter for a different venue.

## Inputs

1. **The rejection or decision letter**, including the reviewer reports it references.
2. **The manuscript**, to check what it actually says against what the decision or a review claims.
3. **The author's stated grounds** for appeal, in their own words.

If the grounds are vague or amount to disagreement rather than a checkable error, say so before drafting, and ask what specific error or oversight the author believes occurred.

## Letter structure

1. **Header.** Manuscript ID, title, and journal, addressed to the handling editor by name, or the editor-in-chief if no handling editor is named or reachable.
2. **Respectful opening.** Thank the editor for their and the reviewers' time, acknowledge the decision plainly (do not minimize or dispute it in the opening), and state the request, a reconsideration of the decision.
3. **Grounds for appeal, stated up front.** Say clearly and specifically, in a sentence or two, what the grounds are (the factual error, the missed content, the procedural issue, or the reviewer error) before going point by point. The editor should know the shape of the case before reading the detail.
4. **Focused, evidence-based response to the decisive points.** Address the specific points that drove the rejection, not every minor comment. Concede any point that is genuinely valid; do not contest something just because it was raised. For each contested point, cite the manuscript (page, section, figure) or the literature as evidence; never assert without a pointer to real support.
5. **What the authors would revise if reconsidered**, if applicable. State any changes the authors are willing to make in a fresh round, so the editor sees a path forward, not just a complaint.
6. **Respectful close.** Request reconsideration or a fresh assessment (by the same or different reviewers, as appropriate), and explicitly defer to the editor's judgment on how to proceed.

## Tone and cautions

- Formal, respectful, non-emotional, and evidence-first throughout. This is a rebuttal of a decision, not of the reviewers as people.
- Never attack a reviewer's competence or motives in the letter itself.
- Refer to referees only by the anonymized designation the journal assigned (Reviewer 1, Reviewer 2, ...), never by a real name, even if the author believes they know who the reviewer is — review is normally double-blind for a reason, and naming a suspected reviewer in an appeal is itself a red flag, not a neutral detail.
- If the user insists on personal criticism, an accusatory tone, or naming a reviewer after this guidance, decline to include it in the letter. Redirect any genuine COI or conduct concern to the private editor note described below, and offer the scientific-only version of the letter instead of complying with the request as stated.
- Keep it concise. A long appeal reads as grasping; a focused one reads as confident.
- If there is a genuine concern about a reviewer's conflict of interest or misconduct, raise it privately to the editor as a separate, factual note, not as a public accusation inside the appeal letter.
- Most journals grant one appeal per decision. Treat this as the one chance to make the case well, not a first draft to be revised after a reply.
- Argue the case scientifically, exactly as if resubmitting the paper itself — not emotionally. The evidence must do the work, not the tone.

## Anti-fabrication

Cite only real evidence: actual manuscript locations, actual reviewer text, actual references. Never invent a page number, a quotation, or a citation to make the case look stronger. Where something needed to make a point (a specific page reference, a missing detail from the author) is not available, insert `[[NEEDS AUTHOR INPUT: <what is missing>]]` rather than filling the gap.

## Output

A single formal appeal letter following the structure above, starting from the template in `assets/appeal-letter.md`. Unlike the Draft-mode response package, Appeal mode produces this one document, addressed to the editor, not a point-by-point reviewer response.

## Real examples: how not to appeal, and how to

A calibration-grade contrast between the two extremes.

**How not to appeal** (a real example): "Dear Editor, You and the referees are idiots. I have already published 5 papers in Nature, and Robert Hooke himself appeared to me and said that this is my best yet... I give plenary/keynote lectures at international meetings on the average of every three to four weeks... NEVER has there been an instance when this work has failed to excite the audience. Jaws drop, and eyes pop — every time."

Failure modes on display: insulting the editor and referees directly; arguing from personal authority and anecdote instead of evidence; and reasoning about journal fit from raw paper-count statistics about the journal rather than from anything specific to the actual finding. None of this belongs anywhere near an appeal letter.

**The right way to appeal** (same source, paired contrast): "Many thanks for your informative comments below, and apologies for disturbing you further — I certainly wouldn't bother you if I didn't think we had recently made further observations, of sufficient novelty... Please see attached two high speed movies... which we have adapted our experimental platform to accommodate since our previous submission. We have an extended data set... So my question is, to be quite blunt, would this added context... warrant re-consideration for submission?"

What's different: gratitude and a professional register throughout; a concrete description of new work actually performed since the original submission (not a re-argument of the same evidence the editor already saw); and a direct, specific, blunt question the editor can actually answer. This is the "new data that changes the picture" ground above, executed the way it should read.
