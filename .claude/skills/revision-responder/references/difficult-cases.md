# Difficult Cases

Read this at Step 4, only for the specific comments that fall into these categories. Most comments are straightforward compliance and do not need this file. These are the situations where a wrong stance can lose an otherwise-winnable revision.

The unifying principle: **be evidence-first and non-defensive.** Peer review is a dialogue, and the letter's job is to get the manuscript through, so even a disagreement stays soft, respectful, and explained. The editor is reading over the reviewer's shoulder. A response that is calm, specific, and generous toward the reviewer wins even when you are disagreeing; one that sounds combative loses even when you are right.

## Infeasible or out-of-scope experiment

A reviewer asks for an experiment you cannot do (no access to the model system, cost or time prohibitive, or it would be a separate study).

Do not simply refuse, and do not silently ignore it. The move is: acknowledge the value of the suggestion, explain the specific constraint honestly, and offer the strongest feasible substitute.

- Provide an alternative line of evidence that addresses the underlying concern (an orthogonal analysis, existing data, a literature precedent).
- If nothing substitutes, add it explicitly to the manuscript as a limitation and a direction for future work.
- Never claim infeasibility for something that is merely inconvenient; reviewers and editors can tell.

Template stance: *"We agree this experiment would further strengthen the mechanistic claim. It requires [specific constraint, e.g. a conditional knockout line that is not available to us and would take ~12 months to generate]. To address the underlying question of [X], we have instead [orthogonal evidence], and we now state this boundary explicitly in the Discussion (p. X)."*

## Reviewer factual error

The reviewer states something incorrect about the paper or the field.

Assume first that the error is really a symptom of unclear writing on your side. If the reviewer misread the paper, the fix is almost always to clarify the manuscript, then note the clarification, rather than to tell the reviewer they were wrong.

- If it is a genuine misreading of your text: clarify the text, and in the response say "We have revised [location] to make this clearer," quoting the clarified text.
- If it is a factual error about the field: correct it politely with a citation, framed as shared understanding, not as a lecture. "We note that [X]; see [ref]. We have added this reference to make the basis explicit."
- Never write "the reviewer is mistaken" as a bare assertion. Show, with a source or a pointer to the data.

## Conflicting reviewers

Two reviewers ask for opposite things (e.g. R1 wants more methodological detail, R2 wants the methods shortened).

- Name the conflict openly and neutrally in both responses; do not pretend it does not exist.
- Find a solution that serves both where possible: move detail to a supplementary methods section (satisfies R1's completeness and R2's request for a leaner main text).
- If the two cannot both be satisfied, make a reasoned choice, explain it in both responses, and let the editor arbitrate. Editors expect this and respect a clearly reasoned choice.

Template stance: *"Reviewer 1 requested additional methodological detail (R1.4) while Reviewer 2 suggested condensing the Methods (R2.2). To satisfy both, we have kept the main-text Methods concise and moved the full protocol, including [detail], to Supplementary Methods S3."*

## Statistical challenge

The reviewer questions a statistical choice, sample size, correction for multiple comparisons, or claims the analysis is inappropriate.

- Take these seriously; they are frequently central and frequently correct.
- If the reviewer is right, redo the analysis correctly and report the new result, even if it weakens a claim. A corrected weaker claim survives; an uncorrected strong claim does not.
- If your original analysis was defensible, justify it with a specific methodological reason and, ideally, a citation for the chosen approach, and consider adding the reviewer's suggested test as a robustness check in the supplement.
- Never hand-wave with "standard in the field" alone; pair it with a reason or reference.

## Framing / significance / novelty objection

The reviewer argues the work is incremental or the significance is overstated.

- This is about positioning, not data, so the fix is usually in the framing: sharpen the specific advance, and, if the claim was genuinely overstated, dial it back rather than defending the overstatement.
- Point to what is concretely new relative to the closest prior work, ideally in one sentence the editor can quote.
- Do not respond with volume or adjectives ("highly novel", "first-ever"); respond with a precise, defensible statement of the delta.

## Compliance objection

Data availability, code, ethics approval, reporting-standard checklists (CONSORT, STROBE, ARRIVE, etc.).

- These are usually non-negotiable and fast to fix: deposit the data, add the accession number, add the statement, complete the checklist.
- If something genuinely cannot be shared (patient privacy, third-party restriction), state the specific governance reason and the controlled-access route.
- Flag anything that requires an action outside the document (a deposition, an IRB reference number) with the placeholder so the author actually does it.

## A reviewer with a conflict of interest or inappropriate feedback

If a reviewer appears to have a conflict of interest, or gave feedback that is inappropriate rather than substantive, this concern goes to the handling editor privately, never into the public response letter.

- Do not air the complaint in the point-by-point; that document is read by the reviewers.
- In the response letter, still address the substantive parts of that reviewer's comments professionally, as you would any other.
- Separately, draft a brief, factual note to the editor raising the concern and asking them to consider it. A change of reviewer is possible only at the editor's discretion, so the ask is for the editor to review the situation, not a demand.

## Appealing or pushing back on the decision

An unfavorable decision, including a rejection after review, is not always final. You do not necessarily have to take no for an answer.

- Where the authors have a genuine, evidence-based case that a decision rested on a misunderstanding, that case can be made respectfully to the editor, with specific reasoning rather than frustration.
- Keep the tone collaborative and the argument grounded in the science; an appeal is still a dialogue, not a complaint.
- Be realistic with the author about the odds and the time cost, and never overstate the strength of an appeal. This is the author's decision to make; lay out the case for and against rather than pushing them toward it.
- For a formal appeal (rebuttal) letter to the editor after a rejection, this is Appeal mode: read `references/appeal-mode.md` for the full grounds, letter structure, and template.
