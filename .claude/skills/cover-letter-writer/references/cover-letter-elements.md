# Cover Letter Elements

Read this when drafting. Each element below has guidance and a standard phrasing you can adapt. Fill real values only; flag anything the author has not supplied with `[[NEEDS AUTHOR INPUT: ...]]`. Never invent a declaration, number, link, or reviewer.

## 1. Professional presentation

Use the corresponding author's institutional email, not a private address, and institutional letterhead (with logo) where available. This is presentation the author applies; note it if they are pasting the text into plain email.

## 2. Salutation

- Generic: "Dear Editor," or "To whom it may concern,".
- Named, if the handling editor is known or has been in contact (more likely to reach the right person): "Dear Dr. Smith," or "Dear [Full Name],".

## 3. Submission statement with manuscript basics

State the title, the submission type (original article, review article, clinical case report, short communication, etc.), the full journal name in italics, the word count, the number of figures and tables, and corresponding-author contact.

Standard phrasing: "On behalf of all authors, I wish to submit our manuscript entitled '[Title]' to be considered for publication in *[Full Journal Name]* as an [submission type]."

## 4. Significance and broad interest

A few distilled sentences on aim, key findings, innovation, and impact. Do not copy the abstract or conclusion; extract the value and highlights. Crucially, make clear why the work matters to readers beyond the immediate subfield: a paper with broad significance is easier for an editor to support than a narrow one, so state the wider relevance explicitly.

Standard phrasing: "This [type] study investigated [question], focusing on [focus]. Our key finding is [finding], which [why it matters broadly]."

## 5. Journal fit

Argue fit against the journal's stated aims and scope. If the journal has published similar work, cite those articles as concrete evidence of fit.

Standard phrasing: "We believe that this manuscript is appropriate for publication in *[Journal]* because it [reason tied to the journal's scope], in line with [journal's prior related articles]."

## 6. Continuity with the authors' prior work

Briefly reference the authors' own related published research to show a track record and continuity. Keep it to a few citations.

## 7. References

Cite appropriately and sparingly; a cover letter should not carry a long reference list.

## 8. Declarations (write the sentence; insert real values or flag)

- **Conflict of interest:** disclose all, or state none. Phrasing: "I am authorized on behalf of all the authors to confirm that no author has any conflict of interest to disclose." (Replace if conflicts exist.)
- **Ethics, consent, trial registration:** state that experiments met ethical requirements, that informed consent was obtained where applicable, and give the clinical-trial registration number if any. Flag missing specifics.
- **Originality and exclusivity:** "The work in this article is original. This manuscript has not been published and is not under consideration for publication elsewhere."
- **Prior dissemination:** if the work was posted as a preprint or presented at a conference or in proceedings, declare it, with the preprint link and the conference details.
- **Data availability:** if raw data are in a public repository, give the specific link (and password if the repository uses one). If deposition is not yet complete — the user says to write the accession as if issued, "I'll deposit it later" — phrase it as pending/embargoed instead: an accession is issued at the moment of submission, not before, so stating it as already done reads as if it exists when it doesn't. Match `submission-screener`'s `data-deposition-guide.md` wording for this if that skill has already established the fact in this session, rather than letting the two skills' phrasing drift apart.
- **All-author approval:** "All the authors have approved the manuscript and agree with this submission."

## 9. Suggested and opposed reviewers

Provide these where the journal invites them.

- **Principle:** suggest genuine experts, typically authors of relevant work cited in the manuscript, not just people the authors know. For each, add a brief justification (a relevant reference) to show relevance. Give institution, address, and email as the journal requires.
- **Opposed reviewers:** name any the authors request be excluded.

Standard phrasing: "If you feel the manuscript is appropriate for your journal, we suggest the following reviewers: [Name, institution, address, email] (relevant work: [reference]). We respectfully request that [Name] not review our manuscript."

## 10. Close and signature

Courteous close, e.g.: "Thank you very much for your kind consideration of our article. Please do not hesitate to contact me if any further information is needed. I look forward to hearing from you."

The signature may include the author's academic titles and positions. Do not, in the body, boast about honors or standing.

## Recommended order

Salutation; submission statement with basics; significance and broad interest; journal fit; continuity; declarations; suggested/opposed reviewers; close; signature.

## The pitch, as one unit

Elements 4 and 5 above (significance/broad interest, and journal fit) are really one continuous pitch, best read as a single arc rather than two separate boxes to fill: very brief context directly relevant to the findings, the key finding itself, why it matters, and why it will interest *this* journal's readership specifically. The last part is the one most often skipped — and it is the one thing that makes this a cover letter rather than a restated abstract.

## Alternative: a compact one-paragraph opening

Some authors prefer the pitch compressed into one flowing paragraph rather than the separated elements above. Both are valid; adapt to the author's preferred style, filling only real values and flagging anything unsupplied with `[[NEEDS AUTHOR INPUT: ...]]`.

Standard phrasing: "We would like to submit our manuscript entitled '[Title]' as a [submission type] in *[Journal Name]*. [To our knowledge, this is the first report showing [finding]. This is important because [reason].] We believe our findings would appeal to the readership of *[Journal Name]* because [reason tied to scope/readership]. Possible referees we would suggest include [Name, institution, relevant work]."
