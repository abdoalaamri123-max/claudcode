# Writing and Tone Rules

Apply these rules after the substantive answer is correct. Do not use polish to conceal an incomplete response.

## Professional, appreciative tone

Assume that the editor and reviewers intend to improve the manuscript, even when a comment feels harsh. Maintain a calm, respectful, polite, and humble tone.

Thank reviewers for consequential concerns, not every sentence. Make appreciation specific:

> Thank you for identifying this ambiguity in our causal logic.

Do not repeatedly write `Thank you for this excellent and insightful comment.` Do not describe every comment as excellent, brilliant, or extremely helpful.

Do not write defensively, attribute motives, or mention frustration, unfairness, or workload. Avoid:

- `The reviewer appears to have missed our point.`
- `The reviewer misunderstood the manuscript.`
- `As we already explained.`
- `We clearly stated.`
- `This comment is incorrect.`

Prefer accountable, forward-moving language:

- `Our original explanation did not make this distinction sufficiently clear.`
- `We appreciate the opportunity to clarify the issue.`
- `We recognize that the original manuscript may have invited this interpretation.`
- `We revised the manuscript to prevent this ambiguity.`

## Use the reviewer's terminology

Follow the reviewer's preferred label when it accurately describes the issue and does not distort the theory. This reduces cognitive load and signals understanding.

- Use `placebo test` if the reviewer uses that term; do not rename it `supplementary falsification analysis` without a methodological reason.
- Do not alternate among `initial status`, `baseline standing`, `prior rank`, and `preexisting status` for the same construct.
- Maintain consistent terminology across the response, manuscript, tables, figures, appendices, and supplements.
- When the reviewer's label is inaccurate, explain the distinction politely, agree on a mutually understandable term, and use the manuscript's canonical label consistently.
- Do not invent new words, constructs, or labels merely to answer a comment.

## Avoid negative framing

Do not organize the response around blame, failure, burden, or what the reviewer missed. Use one accountable clause when needed, then move to the remedy and its benefit.

Prefer:

> Our original wording left the boundary between X and Y ambiguous. We now define both constructs and explain their distinct roles in the mechanism (p. XX).

Or:

> We now distinguish X from Y at the start of the theory section and use the two terms consistently throughout the manuscript (pp. XX-XX).

Do not use positive framing to hide null results, genuine limitations, or disagreement. Accuracy outranks positivity.

## Prefer active verbs to abstract nouns

Prefer:

- `The constructs differ`, not `There are differences between the constructs`.
- `We distinguish status from power`, not `We provide a differentiation between status and power`.
- `We revised the theory`, not `A revision to the theory was conducted`.
- `We tested the alternative explanation`, not `A test of the alternative explanation was undertaken`.

Use concrete verbs such as clarify, define, distinguish, test, compare, estimate, revise, report, narrow, remove, add, and acknowledge.

Search for avoidable nominalizations such as differentiation, examination, consideration, clarification, revision, and evaluation. Replace them with verbs when this makes the sentence shorter and more precise.

## Prefer active voice when it clarifies action

- `We added the robustness test to Table X`, not `A robustness test was added`.
- `We narrowed the claim`, not `The claim was narrowed`.
- `We report the result in the supplement`, not `The result is reported in the supplement`.

Retain passive voice when the actor is irrelevant and the sentence is clearer. Do not enforce active voice mechanically.

## Keep language direct and precise

- Replace vague nouns such as issue, aspect, thing, and matter with the specific construct, claim, analysis, or ambiguity.
- Do not use vague boilerplate such as `We addressed this issue`, `We considered the concern`, `The manuscript has been revised accordingly`, or `The results remain similar`.
- Do not use dashes. Rewrite the sentence directly.
- Avoid long parenthetical statements.
- Keep sentences readable.

## Do not overwrite

- Do not answer only with gratitude.
- Do not restate the full comment before answering it.
- Do not summarize an entire literature when one distinction resolves the concern.
- Do not duplicate the same detailed answer under several comments.
- Do not paste long revised-manuscript passages.
- Do not begin every response with thanks.

## Final language audit

For every response, ask:

1. Does the first sentence reveal the answer or action?
2. Does the response use the reviewer's accurate terminology and stable manuscript labels?
3. Does it foreground the remedy without hiding negative evidence?
4. Can a nominalization become a precise verb?
5. Can a vague passive become a clear active statement?
6. Is each expression of gratitude specific and necessary?
7. Are vague nouns, dashes, long parentheses, boilerplate, repetition, and excessive quotation removed?
