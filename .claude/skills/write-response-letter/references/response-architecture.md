# Scoped Comment Response Architecture

Use a minimum-sufficient-response principle for each comment in the user's stated scope. Evaluate every substantive response against all seven source elements below, but include only the elements that apply to that comment. Do not mechanically expand a simple response or combine distinct comments into an omnibus answer.

## Mandatory core

Every response must begin with a direct answer and let the reviewer determine:

1. What is the direct answer or position?
2. What did the authors do, or why did they not take the requested action?
3. Where can a change or result be verified, when applicable?

Use only author-provided or artifact-verified definitions, rationales, findings, and changes. If an essential element is missing, insert `AUTHOR INPUT REQUIRED` rather than converting a plausible inference into an author claim.

For every substantive response, explicitly consider:

1. direct answer;
2. interpretation of the concern;
3. conceptual or theoretical issue;
4. new empirical analysis, if any;
5. meaning of the results, if any;
6. exact manuscript change;
7. page, table, or figure location.

Omit an element from the prose only when it is genuinely inapplicable or already handled by a precise cross-reference. The seven-element check remains mandatory even when the final response is short.

## Response depth gate

### Level 1: Minor and fully resolved

Use one or two sentences for typographical corrections, citations, labels, formatting, table notes, and small clarifications with an obvious remedy. Appreciation is optional.

> We now define X at its first use and use this term consistently throughout the manuscript (p. XX).

### Level 2: Substantive but straightforward

Use when the concern requires explanation or meaningful revision but the authors agree with the diagnosis and the remedy is clear. Include only what is relevant:

- direct answer;
- concise interpretation;
- conceptual or empirical rationale;
- manuscript change or evidence;
- location.

> We agree that the original manuscript did not clearly distinguish X from Y. We now define both constructs, explain why the distinction matters for the mechanism, and use the labels consistently in the theory and measures sections (pp. XX-XX).

### Level 3: Major, contested, or evidence-intensive

Use the fuller architecture for decision-critical, multi-part, theoretical, empirically complex, alternative-explanation, null-result, infeasible, claim-changing, or disagreement comments. Include only relevant elements, usually in this order:

1. direct answer or position;
2. interpretation of the concern;
3. conceptual or theoretical reasoning;
4. empirical evidence or new analysis;
5. what the evidence means and does not establish;
6. manuscript revisions;
7. exact locations;
8. remaining limitation or tradeoff.

Possible sequence: brief appreciation, direct answer, conceptual reasoning, empirical evidence, manuscript revision, and location. Do not add appreciation or empirical analysis when they are unnecessary.

## Lead with the answer

Do not make the reviewer search. Begin with a clear position such as:

- `We agree.`
- `We agree with the central concern, although we reached a different conclusion about the proposed remedy.`
- `Yes. The results remain consistent under the alternative operationalization.`
- `No. We do not find evidence that the effect differs between the two groups.`
- `We respectfully disagree that these constructs should be combined.`

Then give the reasoning. Do not begin with several paragraphs of background, bury an unfavorable result, or substitute vague claims such as `We addressed this issue`, `We considered the concern`, or `The manuscript has been revised accordingly.` State what the authors did and found.

## Explain manuscript changes precisely

Do not merely say that the manuscript was revised. Identify:

- what changed;
- why it changed;
- how it resolves the concern;
- the exact page, section, table, figure, appendix, or supplement location.

Use explicit location formats:

- Main manuscript: `We revised the theoretical development to distinguish status from power (p. XX).`
- Online Supplemental Materials: `We report the complete results in Table X (p. XX in the Online Supplemental Materials).`

Refer to tables and figures by number. Use visible placeholders such as `Table X`, `Figure X`, `Appendix X`, or `Study X` during drafting and replace all before submission.

When a revision is substantial, quote the most important new text so the editor can see how the key issue was resolved, unless the current journal or editor instructions discourage quotation. Keep quotations selective; never paste several pages of revised text.

## Be concise without being superficial

Give major concerns enough reasoning and keep minor responses brief. Remove background that does not contribute to the answer. Do not summarize an entire literature unless required, paraphrase the comment for several paragraphs, or repeat the same response under multiple comments.

Use cross-references for repeated issues while still answering the local question directly. Response length should track importance and complexity, not a fixed template.

## Respectful disagreement

Authors need not accept every recommendation. Do not agree superficially and avoid the requested change.

For disagreement:

1. identify the valid concern;
2. state the point of agreement;
3. state the precise disagreement clearly;
4. explain why the proposed remedy creates a theoretical, empirical, or interpretive problem;
5. offer an alternative revision;
6. explain how the alternative addresses the underlying concern.

Do not soften the disagreement until the position becomes unclear. Avoid vague phrases such as `We took a somewhat different approach.`

## Misunderstandings require manuscript repair

When a reviewer misunderstands a construct, hypothesis, method, or result, do not rely on the response alone. State that the original presentation invited ambiguity, explain the intended distinction, and revise the manuscript so future readers do not repeat the interpretation.

## Theory before analysis

When a comment raises a conceptual concern, address the theoretical logic before presenting an empirical test. Do not use additional analysis as a substitute for reasoning.

As relevant, clarify:

- why the issue matters for the theory;
- assumptions connecting the constructs;
- proposed mechanism;
- level and unit of analysis;
- temporal ordering;
- boundary conditions;
- distinction from related constructs;
- why the focal account better fits the theory than an alternative.

Then describe the empirical evaluation. Use analysis to support the theory, not to create the argument after seeing results. Revise a legitimate theoretical limitation rather than trying to statistically eliminate it.

## Alternative explanations

Take alternatives seriously:

1. determine whether the alternative is conceptually distinct from the proposed mechanism;
2. state the observable pattern the alternative predicts;
3. state the pattern the focal theory predicts;
4. identify a diagnostic analysis, measure, manipulation, outcome, moderator, or boundary condition.

Do not claim that one nonsignificant coefficient disproves an alternative. Explain the test's evidentiary value and limitations. When appropriate, state that evidence reduces concern rather than eliminates every related explanation.

## New analyses

For every new analysis, explain why it addresses the concern. Report, when relevant:

- sample;
- operationalization;
- model;
- dependent variable;
- added controls;
- focal result;
- coefficient, standard error, confidence interval, significance level, effect size, or other relevant statistic;
- whether the conclusion changes;
- exact result location.

Do not report only that results are `similar` or `robust`; state what remained similar and provide relevant statistics. Report null and unfavorable findings honestly. Explain weakened evidence, narrow unsupported claims, and never hide an analysis because it is inconvenient.

If the rationale, model specification, or interpretation is unavailable, request it explicitly instead of inventing it.

## Separate theory, evidence, and interpretation

State separately what theory predicts, what data show, and how the authors interpret the pattern. Do not claim that a statistical analysis proves a theoretical mechanism.

Calibrate language to the design. Stronger claims may be appropriate when a design directly manipulates the mechanism and rules out alternatives; correlational or indirect evidence requires caution.

Prefer formulations such as:

- `The findings are consistent with our account.`
- `The results provide support for the proposed prediction.`
- `The pattern reduces concern about this alternative explanation.`
- `The analysis does not support the proposed distinction.`
- `The available evidence cannot fully distinguish these explanations.`

Avoid claims that the analysis conclusively proves the theory, makes an alternative impossible, or renders the reviewer's concern unfounded.

## Do not overstate the revision

- Do not describe a minor wording change as a major theoretical revision.
- Do not claim complete resolution from partial evidence.
- Do not say the reviewer requested something that was only suggested.
- Do not call a result unchanged when its coefficient, significance, or interpretation changed meaningfully.
- Do not call directionally similar findings identical.
- State limitations transparently and revise the manuscript's limitations and future-directions discussion when appropriate.

## Show how the revision improves the paper

Do not frame the change only as compliance. Explain how each revision improves the manuscript, connecting it as appropriate to:

- sharper theoretical contribution;
- more coherent study package;
- greater confidence from new evidence;
- more precise claims;
- clearer boundary conditions and explanatory power.

Keep this proportional. For a minor edit, the improvement may be evident in the direct statement of the correction rather than a separate paragraph.
