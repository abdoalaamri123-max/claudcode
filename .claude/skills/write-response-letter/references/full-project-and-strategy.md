# Full-Project Context and Revision Strategy

Read this file completely before drafting the first response for a project.

## 1. Central objective

Do not try to prove that the reviewers were wrong. Make it easy for the editor and reviewers to conclude that the manuscript improved enough to merit publication.

Treat the response as scholarly reasoning, not an administrative change log. Demonstrate that the authors:

- understood the concern;
- took it seriously;
- made thoughtful and consequential changes;
- produced a stronger theoretical and empirical contribution.

Expect the response letter to shape how reviewers read the revision. Do not promise acceptance. Reduce uncertainty, address underlying concerns, and facilitate the editor's decision.

## 2. Read the entire project before drafting

Begin by inventorying and reading every file in the project folder. Do not silently exclude a file before determining what it contains. The project materials include:

- original manuscript;
- revised manuscript, if available;
- editor's decision letter;
- every reviewer report;
- previous response letters;
- original submission letter;
- analysis files and statistical output;
- tables and figures;
- online supplemental materials;
- measures, stimuli, coding protocols, and preregistration documents;
- author notes and meeting summaries about revision decisions.

Understand the primary and supplemental analyses, robustness tests, measures, manipulations, exclusions, samples, and findings. Check whether the authors already made a relevant change that is not yet described in the response.

Check consistency in terminology, page numbers, table and figure numbers, sample sizes, coefficients, and significance levels. Never claim an analysis was conducted unless it was conducted and verified.

During drafting, use complete empirical sentences with visible placeholders when necessary, for example: `The interaction remained significant, beta = XX, SE = XX, p = XX.` Replace every placeholder with verified information before submission.

## 3. Use target-journal and manuscript-type standards

Identify the target journal and consult its current instructions or relevant journal-specific skill. Give current journal expectations priority over generic advice, but do not preload a fixed journal mode.

Consider how the journal evaluates:

- theoretical contribution;
- conceptual clarity;
- construct validity;
- causal inference;
- research design;
- empirical rigor;
- practical relevance;
- generalizability;
- transparency;
- incremental versus substantial contribution.

Adapt the response to the manuscript type:

- **Theory:** conceptual precision, logical coherence, explanatory mechanisms, boundary conditions, and novelty.
- **Quantitative empirical:** theory, identification, measurement, model specification, robustness, and effect-size interpretation.
- **Experiment:** construct and manipulation validity, internal validity, alternative mechanisms, and theory-manipulation fit.
- **Archival:** measurement, sample construction, identification, omitted variables, specification, and alternative explanations.
- **Qualitative:** theoretical sampling, analytic transparency, coding, evidence, interpretive rigor, and data-theory connection.
- **Mixed methods:** explain how the studies complement one another rather than treating each as an isolated answer to criticism.

## 4. Diagnose explicit and underlying concerns

Read each comment literally and infer the uncertainty that may motivate it. Treat the inference as provisional.

Common mappings include:

- a requested control may reflect doubt about the causal mechanism;
- a measure question may reflect an unclear construct definition;
- a request for another study may concern generalizability, internal validity, or construct distinction;
- an unclear contribution may reflect doubt that the theory changes existing understanding;
- an alternative mechanism may signal that current evidence is not diagnostic;
- a request to cite another literature may reflect unclear conceptual boundaries.

Before drafting individual responses, summarize themes across reviews, such as:

- unclear theoretical contribution;
- insufficient construct differentiation;
- unestablished causal logic;
- a design that does not isolate the mechanism;
- claims that exceed evidence;
- an incoherent study package;
- unspecified boundary conditions;
- plausible alternative explanations.

Use these themes to guide revision strategy. Ensure that point-by-point responses collectively resolve the larger concern.

## 5. Prioritize the editor's decision letter

Read the editor's letter before the reviewer reports. Treat it as the primary guide because the editor interprets and prioritizes reviewer input.

Identify:

- reviewer concerns the editor explicitly endorses;
- issues the editor calls essential;
- issues described as optional, secondary, or outside revision scope;
- places where the editor synthesizes several comments into one central problem.

When editor and reviewer guidance appear inconsistent, follow the editor unless a compelling reason requires another course. In a whole-letter package, respond to the editor first and summarize how the revision resolves major concerns; do not merely say, `Please see our responses to the reviewers.`

## 6. Build the response matrix before writing sentences

For every editor and reviewer subquestion, record:

- explicit concern;
- likely underlying concern;
- planned conceptual response;
- planned empirical response;
- manuscript changes;
- relevant page numbers;
- relevant tables and figures;
- unresolved issue;
- grounding status: verified, inferred for planning, or author input required;
- overlap or conflict with other comments.

Classify the response as:

- agree and revise;
- partially agree and revise;
- clarify and revise;
- conduct a new analysis;
- add evidence;
- narrow or soften the claim;
- explain a boundary condition;
- correct a misunderstanding through manuscript revision;
- respectfully disagree;
- explain why a requested analysis or study is infeasible.

Decide which comments require manuscript change and which require explanation only. Most careful-reader misunderstandings should trigger manuscript revision because future readers may have the same problem.

## 7. Handle conflicting recommendations

Identify incompatible reviewer requests and check whether the editor chose a direction. Do not create a conceptually inconsistent manuscript to satisfy both.

Explain the tension respectfully, state the chosen approach and rationale, and show how it addresses the underlying concerns on both sides. Prefer a globally coherent revision over local compliance.

## 8. Decision-facilitation principle

Make major improvements easy to identify, evidence easy to verify, remaining limitations easy to evaluate, and author reasoning easy to follow. Do not overwhelm the editor with volume. Reduce ambiguity.

Aim to leave the editor able to conclude that the authors understood the concerns, made meaningful changes, reported evidence transparently, and produced a substantially stronger manuscript.
