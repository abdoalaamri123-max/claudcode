# Correction Classification (COPE-grounded)

Read this for the full decision framework behind Step 2 in `SKILL.md`. The four possible outcomes are not interchangeable — each carries a different responsibility attribution and a different published wording. Work through them in order below and stop at the first one that applies.

**Version caution:** this reflects the Committee on Publication Ethics' (COPE) long-standing published Retraction Guidelines and standard journal practice. COPE's core criteria are stable, but confirm the target journal's own correction/retraction policy (most publishers state it directly in their author guidelines or ethics pages) for anything procedural or time-sensitive.

## 1. Corrigendum (sometimes called "Correction") — the authors' own error

**Criterion:** an honest mistake made by the authors themselves that does not undermine the paper's overall validity or conclusions.

Typical triggers:
- A mislabeled or duplicated figure panel introduced by the authors.
- A transcription or data-entry error in a table or the text (a value copied incorrectly from a source).
- An incorrect reagent catalog number, antibody clone, cell line identity, or software version.
- An incorrect author affiliation, or an omitted acknowledgment or funding source.
- A miscalculated value that does not change the paper's conclusions once corrected.

Who requests it: the authors, via a letter to the handling editor.

## 2. Erratum — a publisher/production error

**Criterion:** an error introduced by the journal or publisher during production or typesetting, not the authors' mistake, that does not undermine the paper's overall validity or conclusions.

Typical triggers:
- A typesetting error that changes meaning (a dropped negative sign, a misplaced decimal point, a garbled symbol).
- A figure rendered at the wrong resolution, cropped incorrectly, or mislabeled during production.
- An author name, affiliation, or funding statement dropped or altered during typesetting.
- A DOI, pagination, or volume/issue error.

Who requests it: often the authors flag it, but responsibility — and the notice's wording — sits with the publisher. If `proof-checker` was run against the proof before publication and this exact issue would have been a principal-severity finding there, that is strong evidence the error is production-side, not the authors'.

## 3. Expression of Concern (EoC) — credible, unresolved doubt

**Criterion:** credible reason exists to doubt the reliability of the paper's findings, but the matter is not yet resolved. The case does not (yet) meet the bar for retraction, and may later resolve to a Corrigendum/Erratum (if ultimately benign) or a Retraction (if confirmed serious).

Typical triggers:
- An institutional or journal investigation into a possible integrity issue is underway but not concluded.
- Co-authors dispute the authenticity or provenance of specific data.
- Credible third-party evidence of a problem (e.g. a publicly raised image-integrity concern) has surfaced but not yet been fully investigated.
- The findings appear unreliable, but the responsible institution has not launched — or has been unresponsive to a request for — an investigation.

**Key point:** an EoC does not itself conclude wrongdoing. It is a placeholder that tells readers "this is under active question" while resolution is pending.

## 4. Retraction — confirmed and invalidating

**Criterion (COPE's published bases for retraction):** clear evidence exists of one or more of the following:
- The findings are unreliable, either because of a major error (e.g. a miscalculation or an experimental error) or because of fabrication or falsification.
- Plagiarism or redundant/duplicate publication.
- Unethical research (e.g. no proper ethical approval, no informed consent where required).
- Compromised or unreliable peer review.
- A legal issue (e.g. copyright infringement) that cannot otherwise be resolved.

**Key point (from COPE):** a retraction notice must be specific and informative about what happened and why — never euphemistic or vague ("for administrative reasons") when the actual cause is known. It should also state clearly who is issuing the retraction (the authors, the institution, or the editor/publisher).

## Decision flow, in order

1. Is it the authors' own honest mistake, and do the paper's conclusions still hold once it's fixed? → **Corrigendum**.
2. Is it the publisher's own production mistake, and do the conclusions still hold? → **Erratum**.
3. Is there credible doubt about reliability that is not yet resolved (an ongoing investigation, disputed data, an unconfirmed allegation)? → **Expression of Concern**.
4. Is there now confirmed evidence of a major invalidating error, misconduct, unethical research, or compromised peer review? → **Retraction**.

If none of these cleanly fits, or the case sits near the boundary between "serious Corrigendum" and "should be an EoC or Retraction," or fabrication/falsification/plagiarism/duplicate publication is even plausibly in play, do not resolve the ambiguity here: flag it and recommend the corresponding author route the case through the institution's research-integrity office and/or COPE's own guidance. A misconduct determination is an institutional and often legal matter, not a drafting skill's call.

## A recurring signal: the same type of error across multiple papers by one author

The decision flow above is written per-paper, but a genuinely different situation arises when the *same kind* of error (e.g. a duplicated or mismatched image) turns up in more than one paper sharing an author, especially the same lead or corresponding author. Recurrence across independent works is itself an escalation signal that real research-integrity investigations key on, distinct from any single instance's own severity — a pattern is evidence a single Corrigendum-level classification cannot capture on its own.

If the case being discussed appears to be one instance of such a pattern (the user mentions, or you notice, that the same issue has come up before for the same author), do not simply run the decision flow independently for each paper and issue separate Corrigenda. Flag the recurrence explicitly, note that it changes the appropriate response from "fix this one instance" to "this pattern itself warrants institutional review," and recommend routing the whole pattern — not just the paper at hand — through the institution's research-integrity office, per the ambiguity clause above.

## A related but distinct case: authorship changes after publication

An author who should have been included, excluded, or reordered, discovered after publication, is usually corrected via its own Corrigendum (sometimes a dedicated "Author Correction"), not treated as a scientific error. Deciding who should actually be an author is `authorship-credit`'s job; once that's settled, this skill's Corrigendum workflow drafts the notification and notice for the published record.
